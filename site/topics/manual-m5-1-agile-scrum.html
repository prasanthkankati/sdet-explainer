5.1 Agile & Scrum Framework: Roles, Ceremonies & Artifacts

1. The Base: What is Agile vs Scrum and Why Do They Exist?

Agile is an iterative software development and delivery methodology—formalized by the Agile Manifesto's 4 Core Values and 12 Principles—that ships working, tested software in short increments driven by continuous stakeholder feedback. Scrum is the most widely adopted lightweight Agile framework that organizes engineering delivery into fixed time-boxes called Sprints (typically 2 weeks) using 3 Accountabilities (Roles), 5 Events, and 3 Artifacts.

The Problem They Solve: Traditional Waterfall locked requirements for 9 months before delivering a testable build—so when market needs changed in Month 4, the final release was obsolete on launch day, and QA had to compress 9 months of verification into the final 2 weeks. Agile + Scrum deliver a tested, potentially shippable product increment every 2 weeks.

The 4 Agile Manifesto Values

  1. Individuals and interactions over processes and tools — direct engineering collaboration (e.g., Three Amigos: PO + Dev + QA) resolves ambiguity faster than asynchronous ticket handoffs.
  2. Working software over comprehensive documentation — passing automated builds and verified features outweigh static, outdated specification documents.
  3. Customer collaboration over contract negotiation — continuous Sprint Review feedback prevents building the wrong behavior.
  4. Responding to change over following a rigid plan — backlog reprioritization at Sprint boundaries adapts to shifting business requirements without derailing delivery.

The 12 Agile Principles

  1. Early & Continuous Delivery: Satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable, tested software.
  2. Welcome Changing Requirements: Harness requirement changes even late in development for the customer's competitive advantage.
  3. Frequent Delivery: Deliver working software frequently—from every couple of weeks to a couple of months, preferring the shorter timescale.
  4. Daily Business & Engineering Alignment: Product/business stakeholders and developers (including QA/SDETs) must collaborate daily throughout the project.
  5. Empowered Engineering Teams: Build projects around motivated engineers; provide the environment, tooling, and trust required to execute.
  6. Face-to-Face Communication: Direct synchronous conversation is the most efficient method of conveying requirements and defect context.
  7. Working Software as Progress Metric: Working, verified software—not task completion percentages—is the primary measure of progress.
  8. Sustainable Pace: Agile processes promote sustainable development; sponsors, developers, and QA should maintain a constant pace indefinitely.
  9. Technical Excellence & Good Design: Continuous attention to clean architecture, refactoring, and automated test suites enhances agility.
  10. Simplicity: Maximize the amount of unnecessary work not done—avoid over-engineering features or brittle over-mocked tests.
  11. Self-Organizing Teams: The best architectures, requirements, and test strategies emerge from self-organizing cross-functional teams.
  12. Regular Inspection & Adaptation: At regular intervals (Sprint Retrospective), the team reflects on how to become more effective and adjusts its engineering process accordingly.

2. The 3 Scrum Roles (Accountabilities)

  1. Product Owner (PO): Owns the WHAT and WHY—manages and prioritizes the Product Backlog, defines User Stories & Acceptance Criteria, and represents business/customer ROI.
  2. Scrum Master (SM): Owns the Scrum Process—a servant-leader who coaches the team on Agile, facilitates ceremonies, shields the team from mid-sprint scope creep, and removes blockers/impediments.
  3. Developers (Cross-Functional Delivery Team — Devs + Manual QA + SDETs): Owns the HOW—self-organizing engineers who design, code, test, automate, and deliver a "Done" Increment every Sprint. (Scrum recognizes no sub-titles inside the delivery team: QA/SDET is an equal Developer accountability.)

4. The 3 Scrum Artifacts & Their Commitments

Scrum ArtifactCommitmentWhat It Contains
1. Product BacklogProduct GoalSingle prioritized ordered list of all features, bugs & tech debt owned by the PO
2. Sprint BacklogSprint GoalSelected User Stories + action plan committed by the Developers/QA for the current Sprint
3. Product IncrementDefinition of Done (DoD)Sum of all completed, tested, integrated stories meeting quality standards at Sprint end

Interview Answer: "Scrum has 3 Roles (Product Owner, Scrum Master, and cross-functional Developers including QA/SDET), 5 Events (The Sprint container, Sprint Planning, 15-min Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, and Sprint Retrospective), and 3 Artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, and Increment—each tied to Product Goal, Sprint Goal, and Definition of Done)."

5. In Selenium & SDET Automation

  • In-Sprint Automation (Definition of Done): Under Scrum's Definition of Done (DoD), a User Story is not complete until its Selenium Page Object + TestNG regression cases pass in CI alongside manual exploratory verification.
  • Sprint Ceremonies & CI Feedback: SDETs flag locator/API contract changes during Backlog Refinement, report flaky CI runs in Daily Scrum, and resolve recurring test-environment bottlenecks in Sprint Retrospective.
